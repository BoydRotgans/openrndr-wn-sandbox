## The central proposal

Build one story in four chapters, with dinner as part of its rhythm. Each chapter answers a practical question, demonstrates something concrete, and creates a reason to return after the course. The audience should leave with one connected idea rather than four separate talks.

Proposed story question: **How can decisions made today give a building and its components more value tomorrow?** Confirm that wording against WN’s intended message and the actual capabilities presented.

Create interest through discovery, contrast, relevance, and clear progress. Avoid assuming a universal attention-span limit or adding motion on a timer. A quiet dinner interval is a useful release, not a failure to hold attention.

## Principles behind the plan

Connect new material to situations the audience recognises, make its practical relevance clear, and organise it around a small number of relationships. These principles are supported by Carnegie Mellon’s synthesis of learning research; applying them to this dinner event is a design inference, not evidence that a particular running time guarantees attention. [Learning principles](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/principles/learning.html)

Use short recaps at chapter boundaries so guests can re-enter after conversation or service. W3C recommends concise summaries that identify the main points of longer content. The dinner handover method below is our proposed application. [Summary guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o3p08-summary-provided/)

## Four-chapter story spine

Chapter names below follow the supplied notes; check the final approved spelling before production. Course labels describe positions in the evening, not an assumed menu.

| Segment | Audience question | Narrative job | Evidence or reveal | Course connection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arrival | What are these elements going to become? | Establish curiosity through components and drawing | Catalogue forms gradually become recognisable | Atmospheric; no essential information guests can miss |
| 1 — De wereld van bouwen | What can we already organise and build together? | Establish concrete capability and relevance | One verified project or production-chain example | Catalogue gallery: components of the world just introduced |
| 2 — Waardekader en verantwoordelijkheid | How do we know which choices improve things? | Move from capability to accountable choices | One clearly explained, verified comparison; distinguish measurement from claims | Shadow wall: a visual echo of consequences, without implying quantitative proof |
| 3 — Beton: ruggengraat en transitie | Where can we change the material and its use? | Turn the question into physical decisions | One component linked correctly to its claim; a clear before/after where supported | Ordered yard: components, material, and assembly |
| 4 — The Circle | What could we do differently on the next project? | Bring the preceding chapters into a practical proposition | A real case study, composed blueprint views, and a supported next step | Circle-related elements for dessert; coherent ending and exit |

Use one recurring component or project as a thread only if it genuinely connects the chapters. Do not imply that unrelated examples belong to the same building. If necessary, use an explicitly illustrative component and separate it from verified case-study evidence.

## Structure within each chapter

Draft each chapter as a sequence of beats before writing full sentences:

1. **Re-entry:** one sentence recalling the previous conclusion and naming the new question.
2. **Concrete situation:** a recognisable project decision, problem, or constraint.
3. **Evidence:** a real example, component, drawing, or verified figure.
4. **Reveal:** show what changes and why it matters. Use a composed view, selective highlight, or comparison.
5. **Resolution:** state the chapter’s one essential takeaway in plain language.
6. **Bridge:** name the next unresolved question and release the room for the course.

Alternate explanation with visual evidence and short moments of reflection. Do not stack several charts, dense explanations, or spectacular animations without a change in purpose. Optional audience participation should be lightweight: a private reflection or table conversation, not a compulsory response at every break.

Give each chapter a core version and optional supporting beats. The core retains its evidence, conclusion, and bridge if service delays shorten the available time. Set durations after the host and catering schedule is known; the existing video export is not the live running order.

## Course handover pattern

### Leaving a chapter

Finish the argument before service begins. Deliver a short takeaway, then an honest question that the next chapter will address. Transition from the fixed slide layout into a full two-wall backdrop related to the chapter just completed. Bring the dinner bed in gently and release attention to the tables.

Do not hide essential facts in an interlude loop or promise a reveal that never arrives.

### During the course

Let the backdrop support conversation. Use slow, coherent visual behaviour without repeated attention-grabbing stings, urgent text, or major brightness changes. The visual loop must tolerate an unpredictable service duration. A conversation question is optional and should not make the course feel like an assignment.

### Returning to the talk

Wait for a real readiness signal from the host or floor manager. Let the host reclaim attention, reduce the dinner bed, then run the chapter opening once. Restore the large chapter title on the left and content on the right. Begin with a one-sentence recap so nobody must remember an exact detail from before dinner.

Decide during rehearsal whether clearance happens before the readiness signal. Avoid speaking key evidence while plates are being distributed or cleared. If the room is not ready, hold the course atmosphere instead of forcing the next chapter.

## Draft Dutch bridge lines

These illustrate tone and structure; they are not approved final copy or claims.

### Opening

“Vanavond kijken we naar de keuzes achter een gebouw: wat we maken, hoe we de impact beoordelen en wat we bij een volgend project anders kunnen doen.”

### Chapter 1 → first course → chapter 2

Close: “We hebben gezien hoe de onderdelen samen een gebouw vormen. Maar hoe beoordelen we de keuzes die daarachter zitten? Daar gaan we na deze gang op in. Eet smakelijk.”

Return: “Voor de pauze keken we naar wat we bouwen. Nu kijken we naar hoe we onze keuzes beoordelen.”

Optional table prompt: “Welke keuze bepaalt volgens u het meest hoe een gebouw later gebruikt kan worden?”

### Chapter 2 → next course → chapter 3

Close: “We hebben gekeken naar hoe we impact beoordelen. Na deze gang maken we het concreet: welke keuzes kunnen we in het materiaal en de onderdelen maken?”

Return: “Van beoordelen gaan we naar veranderen. We beginnen bij het beton en de onderdelen zelf.”

### Chapter 3 → next course → chapter 4

Close: “We hebben naar het materiaal gekeken. Straks brengen we de onderdelen weer samen en bekijken we welke rol The Circle daarin kan spelen.”

Return: “We hebben de onderdelen bekeken. Nu brengen we ze samen in het verhaal van The Circle.”

### Chapter 4 → dessert and exit

Close: “Welke keuze zou u bij uw volgende project opnieuw willen bekijken?”

On-screen proposed headline: **Ontdek The Circle.**

The next spoken sentence and supporting copy must state what the QR destination actually offers. Confirm the destination before promising a demo, download, appointment, or service. Keep the action visible long enough for interested guests, then continue into a calm exit atmosphere. If drawing the QR is retained, allow it to finish and hold a stable, tested code.

## Visual and sound roles

- Slides always retain the chapter title on the left and content on the right.
- Backdrops may occupy both walls as one coordinated experience.
- Catalogue, technical drawing, and sculptural backdrop remain three treatments of the same component family.
- Blueprint case studies use the confirmed sequence of composed views with precise transitions.
- A highlight should identify what the speaker is discussing, not decorate unrelated areas.
- Sound marks underline meaningful arrivals or changes. They need not occur equally often in every chapter.
- The evening can progress from curiosity to confidence and possibility. This is an emotional direction, not a requirement that each successive cue be louder or busier.

## Writing workflow and decision gates

### 1. Confirm the practical promise

Agree on the audience, the one idea they should retain, and the real final action. Confirm the service order, available speaking windows, host, and source of the room-ready signal. Work can begin on the story spine while these details remain unresolved.

### 2. Make a beat sheet

For each chapter, write the question, real example, reveal, takeaway, and bridge. Check that the next chapter answers the question planted before the course. Choose which evidence is core and which is optional.

### 3. Write the Dutch spoken draft

Use language a builder would naturally use. Put supporting detail in speaker notes. Mark unverified facts for WN rather than smoothing them into plausible claims. Keep established proper names unchanged.

### 4. Pair every beat with a screen purpose

Use the following script table:

| Beat | Audience question | Dutch spoken line | Left wall | Right wall / full backdrop | Sound | Advance condition | Evidence status | Core or optional |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: facade detail | Why does this component matter? | Draft after project evidence is selected | Current chapter title | Composed close-up with one highlighted detail | Optional restrained accent | Speaker finishes explanation | WN confirmation required | Core |

### 5. Prototype one complete handover

Build a reviewable sample of the end of a chapter, the course atmosphere, and the next chapter’s re-entry. Include one composed blueprint detail sequence. This tests the evening’s rhythm more usefully than polishing isolated transitions first.

### 6. Rehearse aloud and at projection scale

Check whether a listener can state each chapter’s takeaway, whether the bridges feel natural, whether the host can regain attention, and whether the room can hold if service runs late. Test normal, delayed, and shortened schedules. Refine reading time, contrast, sound balance, and QR usability on site.

### 7. Record decisions

Keep the improvement-intents document as the design source of truth. Log each choice with its purpose, selected treatment, status, and evidence needed. Keep this script plan as a companion until the running order and spoken script are approved.

## Success criteria

Guests can reconnect after each course without having followed every frame. Each chapter has one clear point, supported by trustworthy evidence. Dinner feels like part of the evening rather than an interruption. The visual world remains coherent while the storytelling changes pace. The ending offers a real and understandable next step.
